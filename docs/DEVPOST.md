# Charter — Devpost

## Elevator pitch

An AI finance team that lives in one database: it catches and undoes its own mistakes when later evidence contradicts them, pushes back when a person's verdict would break an agreement, learns binding policies from its losses, and cannot move a cent outside a kernel the AI cannot change. Models only read email and write candidate rules; every decision is a rule program the kernel runs.

## Try it

- Website: https://pinkstrokes.github.io/charter/ — give the team a task (a payment, a bank change, a chase, a rule to learn) and watch it decide on the real history (a replay of a port of the seats' code, in the browser, over the runs' recorded data).
- The live system, read-only for anyone: https://pinkstrokes.github.io/charter/app/ (the three companies replayed on SpacetimeDB maincloud).
- Code, data and the replay caches: https://github.com/pinkstrokes/charter

## Seats it fills

Cash-application accountant, collections specialist and refunds specialist (receivables); payables clerk and payments (payables); mailroom clerk; internal auditor. People: two controllers and field consultants.

## Inspiration

Hyphenate asks what it takes to hire an AI seat: limited access, a clean record, knowing when to ask, and catching what others missed. Finance already answers that for humans with the three lines of defence. We built the AI version, and asked what an AI team must do that a rules engine with a memory of corrections does not: notice when something it decided weeks ago was wrong, say no to a person who is wrong, and turn a loss into a rule that binds every desk.

## Where the AI is

Three jobs, none of them a decision. Two model families read every email that could move money; they hold no tools, what they read is filed as untrusted facts, and when they disagree or either does not answer, the email waits for a person. A model writes candidate versions of a skill from people's corrections. A third family re-reads a sample of emails for the internal auditor. Which invoices a payment pays, whether to chase and whether money may move are decided by small rule programs the kernel runs deterministically; a version a model writes goes live only after a deterministic replay over every past decision and a person's approval.

## What it does

- **Catches its own mistakes.** When a payment names an invoice the team already paid by inference, it works out that its earlier application was a guess, asks the controller with the evidence, reverses it, re-applies both payments and puts the rule that guessed under tightened inspection.
- **Defends the agreement.** A spot check or correction that would chase a counterparty inside its contractual grace period is declined with the reason, never counted against the seat and never learned. A spot-check verdict that contradicts a rule with a clean record is put back to the person (a slip, or does it stand?) before it counts.
- **Checks what no seat owns.** A solver takes the collections skill, the kernel's rules and the grace guard together, over every possible situation. It found that a customer whose own money was waiting to be applied could still be sent a past-due notice, and charged late fees where the contract has them. Two constitution articles now forbid the notice and the fee (C16, C17), and a third forbids fees in bankruptcy (C18), proved for every situation. In the final runs that stopped collections from chasing 7 of company B's customers and 8 of C's with their own money: the kernel refused each notice, collections then held on its own, citing the refusal, and every other chase went through. Runs before the fix, since replaced, had sent those customers 106 such notices.
- **Learns policies from losses.** After a debit bounced during an unverified bank change, an exhaustive replay of every past money movement found the one rule that would have stopped it without holding back anything legitimate; the controller ratified it and it now also stops refunds and vendor payments — for the controller too.
- **Shares what it learns.** A new company can start from the skill a sister company learned, ratified by its own controller; a company with a history replays an offered skill through its own gate and refuses what does not fit.
- **Learns skills from one sentence, safely.** A correction becomes a candidate version of the team's skill, written by a model and judged by a deterministic replay over every past decision. Unsupported rules are dropped, failing candidates repaired, new thresholds centred between the nearest known cases; while nothing passes, the gate's counterexamples go back to the model for another try, and such a revision may only fix a mistake, never take work from people; a person approves.
- **Earns autonomy rule by rule.** A rule acts alone until its record proves, at 95%, that it is wrong more often than tolerated. Spot checks go 25% → 10%; 40% after an error until a long clean run (74 checks at a 2% tolerance); 3% only after a bound valid however often it is checked. These are engineering values, and we say so.
- **Weighs sources.** Who paid is voted by the account on file, the invoice named and the payer's name; two model families read every email that could move money.
- **Takes a judge's own scenario.** On the World screen anyone at the demo makes up a payment (any payer, any invoices, any reference, any account) or a bill; it enters through the same door as everything the simulation delivered, and the screen follows it to the seat's decision, the rule that fired and where that rule was learned, or to the question it asked.
- **Audits itself.** A read-only auditor on another model family tests every control and re-performs a monetary-unit sample each month; the controller closes the month into a certificate that hashes the rules in force, anchored in git with the hash of the program the database was running.

## How we built it

SpacetimeDB holds everything: the kernel and the seats are a TypeScript module (reducers for every command, procedures for the seats that call models), so the ledger, the record and the live UI share one transactional state. Models are quarantined readers through Alibaba Model Studio (Qwen and Kimi as the two readers, Qwen-Max as the skill writer, DeepSeek as the attacker and the auditor's re-reader). The evolver, the auditor, the benchmark harness and the simulated worlds are Node and Python tools that reuse the module's own interpreter, so what is proven offline is what runs online; the competence map and the team checks run Z3 over that same TypeScript. React and the SpacetimeDB client for the UI. Built from Friday 22:00: a spike first (model calls inside procedures do not block the ledger), then the kernel, the seats, company B's real invoices, the evolver and the red team before midnight; autonomy, audit, payables and the UI overnight; the long tail of payments, company C, the public page and the solver's team check on Saturday.

## Proof

- **BenchRec (a Tier-1 institution's real cash account, eval split scored once):** the bank desk started from one rule and learned the rest from 67,740 lines of the institution's reconciliation history through the same evolver and gate as every desk: 81.5% of 32,048 bank lines matched alone at 99.95% precision (12 wrong), clearing the 99.8% floor in each of the eval split's full months. Its day-0 rule matches 84.4% at 99.82% (48 wrong) and misses the floor in two months; six rules written by hand match 83.9% at 99.88% (31 wrong) and fall under the floor in April (99.79%); the matcher shipped with the dataset matches 64.9% at 99.45%. The hand-written rules match 783 more lines for 19 more wrong, about 41 manual matches per mistake avoided; a wrong match hides a real difference until someone finds it, and Charter prices a mistake at 1/tolerable questions (500 here).
- **Company B (real UCI Online Retail II invoices and credit notes; simulated payments with the long tail):** on the locked test split the cash landed where the payer meant for 310 of 314 payments. As run, the team settled 304 alone and was wrong on 3 (95% upper bound 2.5%). Replayed over the same test payments, the day-one skill would be wrong on 18 of the 301 it settles alone; the controller's answers made four replay-gated versions, and the current one is wrong on 2 of 306. From February it settled 98–100% of payments alone each month; twice a later payment exposed an earlier guess and the team undid it.
- **Company A (simulated franchisor, six months, planted errors and people who make mistakes):** on the locked test split under-reported weeks caught 0/4 → 3/4 with all 14 legitimately low-cash, 5 bulk-buying and 88 clean weeks still accepted (in June it also caught one store's milder under-reporting, all three live weeks); cheques in the mail no longer chased 0/3 → 3/3; duplicate bills rejected 0/2 → 2/2; $89,242 of duplicate bills stopped and none paid; one returned debit became three binding policies; the internal auditor qualified two months for duplicate liabilities and a misbilled royalty; fourteen of people's verdicts were put back to them for a second look (nine were slips). The under-reporting desk is the one that moves between runs: its final rule caught 3 and 1 of the 4 test weeks in earlier local runs. Still missed: two short payments that named their invoice, which A's day-one cash skill (it does not read references) applied elsewhere. Company B's skill, offered to company A whole and rule by rule at every position, was refused by A's own gate: it would get three of A's labeled cases wrong and handles none of the cases A's people had just taught.
- **Skills travel (company C, a sister ledger of real 2009–10 invoices, run twice on identical events):** started from the cash skill company B learned (only the program crossed; C's controller ratified it; every rule started on probation), C was wrong 2 times in its first three months when it settled alone instead of 20, asked 34 cash questions in seven months instead of 72, and needed 2 new versions instead of 7; on its locked test split the cash landed where meant for 320 of 324 payments instead of 315. After three months the head start was spent: 15 mistakes in 832 against 11 in 816, in both ledgers mostly money sent on account. C's were applied by rules every skill here starts with (chiefly the hand-written catch-all, apply by contract order, which B's skill had kept) and by a rule C learned from one lucky case, and C's gate would not hand those cases back to people on one labeled mistake when it needs three.
- **Competence map and team checks (`tools/certify`, Z3 over the module's own TypeScript):** its encoding agrees with the interpreter on all 3,840 checks; the cash skill's versions can face 40 situations, none missing, each realized as a concrete payment, and company B's current version would act alone in 11 that none of its 1,225 payments ever fell into. Replayed without hindsight, earning autonomy situation by situation would halve B's mistakes made alone (14 to 7) for 21 more questions. The map is a coverage certificate for the cash skill, not a correctness one; the proof is the team check: C16–C18 hold for every situation and both collections versions, and each of the four other fixes we checked fails at least one property.
- **Red team:** 36 attack emails by another model family; Charter 0/36 with a careless controller, mostly by construction (the models hold no tools, and money moves only to verified accounts after a callback, with two people for large payments and refunds); tool-using LLM agents 13, 8 and 4 of 36 with plain prompts. Not yet tried: forged remittances, poisoning the learning, floods of questions, other attacker families.
- **Workload, counted from the databases:** over B's seven months the controller answered 671 spot checks and 76 questions, approved 4 versions and closed 6 months; after the first two months that is about four spot checks and half a question a business day. Starting from B's skill saved company C 38 questions, 8 corrections and 4 approvals.

## Challenges

Our own evaluator kept catching us. The evolver overfit one example until we chose candidates by robustness; masked bank-account numbers collided and sent a customer's payments to another customer until payer identity became a vote between sources; simulated people's slips asked for notices inside the grace period until the team learned to push back. Making the payments messy broke learning outright: with bank charges in the data no candidate could pass, because "apply to the invoice the payer named" and "apply oldest first" looked like contradictory answers for a customer with one open invoice. Decisions now record what each outcome would do, and everything compares that. A reviewer we asked to attack the gate showed that our first statistical version could pass a candidate wrong on 51 of its 52 new decisions; fixed. A solver, not a run, found that collections could chase a customer whose own money was waiting to be applied; the kernel now computes that fact and the constitution forbids it. Then a reviewer's critique of our claims sent us back in: the seats now take work in arrival order, so local and maincloud runs agree; collections reads its own refusals instead of asking again every day; taking work away from people needs a bound valid however often it is checked; and an email a reader did not answer waits for a person instead of being filed on one reading. One of our fixes failed in the trial run: counting every spot-check error as a precedent let a single planted slip escalate 182 near-identical collections cases in company A, so a spot-check verdict now becomes a precedent only once the person stands by it on a second look. And chasing why our maincloud replay of company A kept taking its own path, we found its evolver had been reading another folder's answer key, so A's locked test cases were not hidden from it on maincloud; local runs were never affected, and the replay now matches. Last, we gave the evolver counterexample rounds: when nothing passes the gate, the cases the gate found against the best candidate go back to the model for another try. Replayed on the final runs' own histories, the loop rescued exactly one refused lesson, and the answer key says it was wrong: the model had narrowed a takeover just enough to step around the one bill where people disagreed. Revisions may now only fix mistakes people found, never take work from them, and with that they change no outcome on those histories.

## What's next

Earning autonomy situation by situation instead of rule by rule: replayed on company B without hindsight, it halves the mistakes made alone (14 to 7) for 21 more questions, and it is what company C's payments on account needed. A seat that proposes applying a customer's own credit against what it has past due, instead of only holding the chase. Facts the seats do not measure yet (a customer's payment history), adversarial tests the red team did not try (forged remittances, poisoned corrections, question floods), more packs on the same kernel, and live connectors to a bank sandbox and an ERP.
